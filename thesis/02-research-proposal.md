# How Founders of Early-Stage Marketing Ventures in Russia Make Their First Hiring Decisions

Oct 30, 2026 · @Oleg

Research proposal. Master in Management, Graduate School of Management, St. Petersburg University. Student: Oleg Ezhkov. Supervisor: Prof. Marina O. Latukha.

## 1. Introduction

A founder who hires a first employee gives away part of the work that, until then, only the founder did. In a venture of two to ten people that decision is hard to reverse cheaply: each early hire sets a working standard for the people who come next, takes a share of a thin budget, and either frees the founder's time or consumes it in training and correction. Rocha and Grilli (2024) note that most start-ups have no formal HR function until they employ 40 to 50 people, so these decisions are made by the founder alone, usually without HR training.

The topic comes from my own practice. I have worked in marketing for about six years, founded a production studio, a marketing service and several agencies, and in May 2020 founded the agency I run today. My first two hires there were a client manager and a specialist responsible for delivering the service. I kept strategy, sales, finance and the agency's own marketing. Later the team grew mostly on the delivery side, plus assistants for sales, content, reporting and analytics. The hires that failed were mostly assistants. Some could not learn the role fast enough after I had already spent weeks training them. Others accepted responsibility in the interview and did not deliver on it, which only became visible during the paid trial month. The rules I drew from these cases (a more structured interview, an explicit view of the person's strengths and weaknesses, and readiness to part ways in the first week) were learned by trial and error. I found no research that would have given them to me in advance.

Research on early hiring explains whether young firms hire, how many people and from which networks (Coad et al., 2017; Rocha and Grilli, 2024; Brymer and Rocha, 2024, 2025). It does not model how a founder makes a specific hiring decision, and its samples come mostly from technology start-ups in Western Europe and the United States. Service ventures such as marketing agencies, and the Russian labour market after 2022, are almost absent.

The study addresses one main question and three sub-questions:

- **RQ.** How do founders of early-stage marketing ventures in Russia make decisions about their first hires?
- **RQ1.** What criteria do founders use to evaluate candidates for their first roles, and how do these criteria change with the founder's experience?
- **RQ2.** How do founders identify and reach candidates for roles in a venture that has no reputation and cannot pay market salaries?
- **RQ3.** How do the founder's background and available resources shape the sequence of decisions from defining a role to onboarding a new hire or letting them go?

The aim is to build an empirically grounded model of the first-hire decision process and to translate it into a practical framework that founders can use before their first hires. The study covers ventures up to two years old, at the pre-seed stage, in marketing services (agencies, SMM and content studios, production projects). "First hires" include staff employees, freelancers and self-employed contractors, as well as partners who join with a share of the business.

## 2. Literature review

The review is organised around the decision itself. Section 2.1 asks what makes first hires different from later ones. Sections 2.2 to 2.5 follow the decision from where candidates come from, through why they agree to join and how the founder chooses, to what happens in the first weeks. Section 2.6 places the problem in marketing services and the Russian labour market, and Section 2.7 draws the gap and the research questions out of the tensions found along the way.

### 2.1 Hiring before HR exists

The starting point of most work on young firms is Stinchcombe's (1965) liability of newness: a new organisation has no track record, no routines and no reputation, so outsiders, including job seekers, cannot judge whether it will survive. Cardon and Stevens (2004), in the review that still frames HRM research on small organisations, add the internal side of the same problem. There is no HR department, recruitment is irregular, and the person who hires is usually the owner, who has many other tasks and little training in selection.

Later research has turned this general picture into more specific findings, and some of them sit uneasily with the idea that early hiring is mainly a problem of attracting people. Coad, Nielsen and Timmermans (2017) use Danish matched employer-employee data on the move from self-employment to being an employer. Only a small share of solo entrepreneurs ever hire, and 28% of those who do take a family member as the first employee. Sales growth tends to come before the first hire rather than after it, which suggests that founders hire in response to workload they can no longer carry, not as a planned step. The most striking result is that subsequent profit gains depend on the founder's education and earlier income much more than on the characteristics of the employee. If this holds more generally, a methodology of first hiring is as much about the founder's capacity to use a new person as about the person.

Rocha and Grilli (2024) move the question from "whether" to "under what conditions". On a sample of Italian innovative start-ups, founders' previous start-up experience and the presence of corporate or university shareholders at founding increase the probability of early hiring. Their explanation combines the resource- and competence-based view (Barney, 1991) with signalling theory (Spence, 1973): experienced founders know how to search for and evaluate candidates, and reputable shareholders send a credible signal to them. The effect, however, appears only when the venture is innovation-oriented, measured by innovation-driven founding motives or substantial R&D spending. Without that orientation, resources and competences do little.

This condition matters for the present study. A marketing agency or an SMM studio is usually not innovation-driven in Rocha and Grilli's sense. It sells a service, often from the first month, and competes on quality of delivery and client communication. Either the founder's resources work differently in such ventures, or they work through mechanisms the innovation-focused samples could not capture. The existing evidence does not allow us to choose between these readings.

A broader limitation is visible in the quarter-century review of HRM in small and medium-sized enterprises by Harney and Alkhalaf (2021). Across 137 peer-reviewed articles they find three recurring weaknesses: studies rarely distinguish between types of small firms, they pay little attention to the conditions in which these firms operate, and they mostly report the views of managers rather than the processes behind decisions. Research on micro-ventures in their first two years, where "HRM" means a founder making a handful of decisions, is a small and under-theorised part of this field. The same review argues that small firms are a productive setting for HRM theory precisely because practices there are informal and visible, which supports a process-oriented, qualitative approach to first hiring.

### 2.2 Where first hires come from

A founder with no employer brand and no recruiter starts from people already within reach. Research agrees that personal and professional ties dominate early hiring. It disagrees on whether this helps the venture.

The strongest recent evidence comes from Brymer and Rocha, who use Danish register data to follow "affiliation-based hiring", that is, recruiting people who share a past employer, school or other affiliation with a founder. In the first paper (Brymer and Rocha, 2024) founding teams whose members share professional ties are more likely to adopt this practice, and once adopted it becomes a routine that persists after founding. The cost is lower diversity of the workforce that later forms. In the second paper (Brymer and Rocha, 2025) the same practice is associated with higher profitability, higher sales and better survival, although the authors note that the benefit depends on the kind of affiliation and on the stage of the firm. Read together, the two papers describe a trade-off. Hiring from known circles lowers the risk of a bad match because the founder has already observed the person at work. It also narrows the range of skills and perspectives in a team that will later need both.

Research on founding teams adds a finer distinction. Lazar et al. (2020) review the literature on entrepreneurial team formation and separate two logics: interpersonal attraction, in which founders choose people they like and trust, and resource seeking, in which they choose people who bring complementary skills. Lazar et al. (2022) then test the combination of the two. Teams formed on both friendship and complementary skills raised more seed funding, did better in an entrepreneurship competition and earned more from first products. The mechanism is a transactive memory system: members know who knows what and coordinate without much explicit negotiation. Only 10% to 15% of the teams they studied used this dual strategy. The rest relied on one logic only.

These results were obtained for co-founders, but two features make them relevant to first employees in a micro-venture. The first employees of a two-person agency work with the founder as closely as co-founders do, and the line between employee and partner is often blurred. In my own practice I have offered a share of the business to an employee when I wanted that person to answer for the result of the whole venture. The question "friend, specialist or both" therefore applies to first hires as well.

The network view is not uncontested. Coad et al. (2017) show that family is the most common source of the first employee, which fits a network account but also suggests that many first hires are made for availability and trust rather than skill. Frid, Wyman and Shaver (2025), studying early firm success, find that neither the founder's human capital (education, industry, managerial or start-up experience) nor the strength of the founder's ties predicts the number of hires in the first year. One way to reconcile these findings is to separate two decisions that the literature tends to merge. Networks may shape *whom* a founder hires much more than *whether* or *when* the founder hires. A second distinction is one the literature has not drawn at all: a founder may hire from personal circles strategically, because the person's work is known, or defensively, because hiring a stranger feels risky. In conversations with less experienced founders, especially students, the second motive seems frequent. The difference matters for a methodology, because the same channel produces different outcomes depending on why it is used.

### 2.3 Why people join an unknown venture

The second half of the matching problem is the candidate's choice. A founder can only select among people who are willing to come, and the literature gives two quite different accounts of who these people are.

The first account stresses preferences. Moser, Tumasjan and Welpe (2017) ran a conjoint experiment with 307 job seekers and identified the dimensions along which a new venture can be an attractive employer despite its size and uncertainty, including the work itself, the team atmosphere, flexibility and the chance to grow with the company. Applicants who behave more entrepreneurially themselves weight these dimensions differently from others, so the attractiveness of a venture depends on whom it addresses. Roach and Sauermann (2024) extend this account with longitudinal survey data on 2,394 science and engineering PhDs. Many of them preferred start-up jobs before entering the labour market, and those who went to start-ups were, on average, of higher ability than those who joined established firms. Start-ups could hire talented people at lower pay because these people valued autonomy and interesting work. On this account, the low salaries of early ventures are not simply a handicap. They act as a filter that leaves the candidates who want what a young venture can offer.

The second account stresses sorting and its costs. Sorenson, Dahl, Canales and Burton (2021) follow Danish workers for ten years after they join a firm. People hired by start-ups end up earning about 17% less than comparable people hired by large established firms. Roughly half of this gap reflects sorting: those who join start-ups bring less human capital to begin with. The rest comes from the high failure rate of young firms, which produces costly spells of unemployment, and from career paths that rarely lead back to well-paying large employers. Early employees bear this penalty most. Dahl and Klepper (2015) point in the same direction from the firm side: founders with stronger pre-entry experience build larger firms, pay more from the start and attract more talented employees.

The two accounts are not necessarily contradictory. They were obtained on different populations, PhD scientists in one case and the whole Danish workforce in the other, and they describe different mechanisms that can operate at once. For a founder, though, they point to a concrete selection problem. A candidate who accepts a low salary may be a motivated person who prefers autonomy and learning, as Roach and Sauermann describe, or a person with fewer alternatives, as the sorting result suggests. The two look alike in an interview.

This problem is sharp in the setting of the present study. My own observation, and the experience of founders I know, is that in Russia in recent years many people are ready to work for modest pay in small marketing ventures, and that among them there are unusually initiative-driven students and early-career people who later become strong specialists. They work remotely, so the region does not matter. Neither strand of research says how a founder can tell these future talents from candidates who simply had no better offer, and how much the answer depends on what happens after hiring rather than before it. The literature on signals (Spence, 1973; Moser et al., 2017) treats the venture as the party that sends signals and the candidate as the one who reads them. In first hiring the founder faces the reverse problem as well, and must read weak signals from candidates with little track record.

### 2.4 How founders decide

Sections 2.2 and 2.3 describe the pool of candidates and the forces that sort people into young ventures. They say little about how the founder chooses within that pool. Four bodies of work offer partial answers, each from a different angle.

**Effectuation.** Sarasvathy (2001) contrasts causation, in which a decision maker starts from a goal and selects the means to reach it, with effectuation, in which an entrepreneur starts from available means (who I am, what I know, whom I know) and lets goals emerge from what these means allow. Seen this way, hiring from personal networks is not a lack of method. It is the method an effectual founder would use, because known people are the means at hand and their commitment reduces uncertainty. Hubner and Baum (2018) bring this distinction into HR research. Interviews with entrepreneurs and new-venture consultants show that founders develop their employees through a mix of the two logics: planned, causal steps when requirements are clear, and effectual steps (using networks, improvising, letting employees shape their own roles) when they are not. The study concerns human resource development rather than selection, but it is one of very few that look inside founders' people decisions, and its main result, that the two logics coexist and switch with circumstances, is directly testable for hiring.

**Simple rules.** Bingham and Eisenhardt (2011) followed six technology ventures from three countries as they entered foreign markets and recorded what they learned. Firms condensed their experience into a small portfolio of heuristics, such as which markets to enter or which opportunities to reject, learned in a characteristic order and becoming more strategic over time. The authors argue that such heuristics are a rational way to act under uncertainty, not a bias to be corrected. The study is about internationalisation, not hiring, but the learning path it describes resembles how founders talk about recruitment: a failed hire, a dismissal, a lesson, and a rule ("do a structured interview", "do not be afraid to part ways in the first week"). If first hiring works this way, a methodology should look like a short set of rules, and founders with more experience should hold more and sharper rules.

**Organisational blueprints.** Baron and Hannan (2002), summarising the Stanford Project on Emerging Companies, found that founders of high-tech start-ups enter with one of five implicit models of employment, based on attachment, the basis of selection and the means of coordination: star, engineering, commitment, bureaucracy and autocracy. The initial blueprint shaped later administrative overhead, turnover and performance, and changing it was destabilising. In this view the founder's first hiring decisions carry a model of the organisation that is hard to revise later, which pulls against the learning account above.

**Cultural imprinting.** Ahn and Greve (2025) show where some of these models come from. Analysing US technology start-ups with natural language processing of employee reviews, they find that founders carry elements of their former employer's culture into the new firm, more strongly when they stayed there longer and when the parent's culture was coherent and unusual. This gives a mechanism for a difference I observe among founders: a founder from a corporation brings a ready template for roles, standards and onboarding, while a student founder has no such template and often hires only acquaintances or avoids hiring altogether.

The four strands disagree on one point that is central to a methodology: whether the founder's approach to people is fixed at founding (blueprints, imprinting) or keeps being rebuilt through experience (simple rules, the mix of causal and effectual logics). Each strand also stops short of the decision this study is about. Effectuation explains where candidates come from but not how they are judged. The heuristics study concerns another domain. Blueprints and imprinting describe a general employment model, not the sequence of choices in a particular hire. None of these studies models the steps a founder goes through from noticing that help is needed to deciding that a new person stays.

### 2.5 Selection, onboarding and early exit

HR research usually treats recruitment, selection, onboarding and dismissal as separate practices, each with its own literature. In a venture of a few people they behave more like stages of one decision. The founder who hires an assistant is also the person who trains that assistant, sees the first week of work and decides whether the trial period ends with a contract or a goodbye.

Several features of early ventures make the boundary between selection and onboarding thin. First, the most important qualities of an early hire are hard to observe before work starts. In my own failed hires the problem was rarely a missing skill that a test task could reveal. It was a person who took on responsibility in conversation and then did not honour it, or who could not learn the role at the speed the venture needed. Both became visible only in the paid trial month. Second, the founder has no separate trainer, so every week of onboarding is a week of the founder's time. A wrong hire costs the salary and also the hours that were supposed to be freed. Third, the first people set the standard that later hires copy. Baron and Hannan (2002) and Ahn and Greve (2025) both imply that what founders do with their first employees imprints the culture, but neither study observes the first weeks of an individual hire.

The literature that does follow learning in hiring decisions comes from online labour markets. Leung (2018) studied employers hiring contract workers on an online platform and found that employers learn from the outcomes of earlier hires and adjust whom they hire next, so hiring itself is an experiential learning process. This setting is close to the reality of small marketing ventures, which often engage freelancers and remote contractors before, or instead of, staff employees. It also supports the view from Section 2.4 that founders' hiring rules are built from experience.

A last element of the decision is new. Digital tools now perform parts of sourcing and screening that a founder once did by hand, from automated filtering of responses to generating test tasks and checking them with language models. Hunkenschroer and Luetge (2022) review the ethics of AI-enabled recruiting and selection and set its promised efficiency and fairness against risks of bias, opacity and loss of human judgement. Their review concerns organisations with HR departments. For a founder without HR staff, the same tools may replace a missing recruiter rather than support an existing one, which changes both the gain and the risk. No study I found examines how founders of micro-ventures use such tools in first hiring.

Taken together, these points suggest that a methodology of first hiring cannot stop at the moment of the offer. A model that includes the trial period, onboarding and the decision to keep or release the person describes the founder's real problem more faithfully, and it is where founders' own lessons tend to concentrate.

### 2.6 Context: marketing services and the Russian labour market

Most of the evidence reviewed so far comes from technology ventures in Denmark, Italy and the United States. Two features of the present setting may change how it applies: the kind of work done in marketing ventures, and the state of the Russian labour market in 2024 to 2026.

**Marketing services as a setting.** Howard-Malek, Kirilova and Sarin (2023) reviewed 71 articles on talent in marketing published in leading marketing and HRM journals since 2000. They conclude that talent management in marketing has received very little research attention and propose a framework (TALMARKS) covering attraction, selection, development, evaluation, reward, succession and measurement. Their focus is the marketing function of larger firms. Small agencies and studios, in which marketing work is the product sold to clients, fall outside it, and I found no journal study from 2021 to 2025 on how such ventures recruit. The work itself has features that matter for selection. Delivery depends on client communication and on individual responsibility for deadlines and quality more than on certified skills, which are the traits founders find hardest to check in an interview. Practitioner surveys also suggest that small agencies rely heavily on freelancers and project contractors, so the first "team" of an agency is often partly outside its payroll.

**The Russian labour market.** The research idea for this thesis started from a common assumption: that early ventures compete for scarce talent. Recent Russian data complicate it. According to HeadHunter's October 2025 review, reported by Kommersant, the average number of active vacancies fell over the year while the number of active CVs grew, and the hh.index (CVs per vacancy) reached 7.3, its highest value since the start of 2023 ([Kommersant, 27 November 2025](https://ancor.ru/upload/files/11.2025-%D0%98%D0%BD%D1%82%D0%B5%D1%80%D0%B2%D1%8C%D1%8E.%20%D0%95%D0%B4%D0%B8%D0%BD%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%BE%D0%B3%D0%B8%20%D0%B8%20%D1%82%D1%80%D0%B5%D0%BD%D0%B4%D1%8B%20%D1%80%D1%8B%D0%BD%D0%BA%D0%B0.%D0%9A%D0%BE%D0%BC%D0%BC%D0%B5%D1%80%D1%81%D0%B0%D0%BD%D1%82.%D0%A1%D0%B0%D0%BB%D0%B8%D0%BA%D0%BE%D0%B2.pdf)). The same source describes a balanced rather than an employer's market and notes demand for "unicorn" profiles that combine marketing with coding or logistics. For a small venture this means that the difficulty lies less in getting applications than in finding, among many of them, the few people who fit an undefined and changing role. Selection, not attraction, becomes the bottleneck.

The demand side is tightening at the same time. The Russian advertising market grew by 8.5% in 2025 to about 981.6 billion roubles, a sharp slowdown after the very fast growth of 2023 and 2024, according to the Association of Communication Agencies of Russia (AKAR) ([ppc.world, 24 March 2026](https://ppc.world/news/tempy-rosta-rossiyskogo-reklamnogo-rynka-rezko-snizilis-v-2025-godu-issledovanie-akar/)). External funding for early ventures is scarce: Russian venture investment was $159 million in 2025, 10% less than a year before, and the number of deals fell by 42% to 102 ([Kama Flow, citing Dsight, 19 February 2026](https://kamaflow.com/en/post/market-in-wait-and-see-mode-results-of-dsights-venture-eurasia-2025/)). Most early marketing ventures are therefore funded by their founders and early clients, which tightens the cost of every hire.

Two further features are specific to Russia. First, the self-employment tax regime (NPD) gives founders a simple legal way to engage people without employing them. The number of registered self-employed grew from 12.2 million at the end of 2024 to about 15.4 million at the end of 2025, and passed 17 million in 2026 ([Interfax](https://www.interfax.ru/russia/1110481)). In practice a "first hire" in a Russian micro-venture is often a self-employed contractor on a trial project, which sits between the freelancer and the employee categories used in Western studies. Second, the Russian talent market has been shaped by emigration and return flows of skilled people. Latukha, Shagalkina, Mitskevich and Strogetskaya (2021) frame talent migration from emerging markets as a challenge that firm-level talent management can partly turn from brain drain into brain gain. Their focus is on larger firms and national talent pools, but their argument that talent management practices are context-bound applies to micro-ventures as well.

Russian-language academic research on how founders hire their first people is almost absent. Searches of Russian management journals produced mainly conference abstracts and practitioner articles. The present study can therefore add an empirical account from a setting that the international literature does not cover and that local research has not yet addressed.

### 2.7 Synthesis: tensions, gap and research questions

The review does not add up to one consistent picture of early hiring, and the places where studies disagree are the most useful for this thesis. Table 1 lists the main tensions and what each implies for the decision a founder faces.

| Issue | One position | Opposing or qualifying position | What remains open for a founder | Addressed by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hiring from personal circles | Improves profit, sales and survival (Brymer and Rocha, 2025) | Reduces diversity and becomes a rigid routine (Brymer and Rocha, 2024); works best only combined with complementary skills (Lazar et al., 2022) | When to hire a known person, and whether the motive is strategic or defensive | RQ2, RQ3 |

**The research gap.** Existing research explains whether young firms hire, how many people, from which networks, and with what consequences for performance and for employees' careers. It does not explain how founders make a specific first-hire decision: how they define the role, whether they choose an assistant, a specialist or a partner, how they search, which signals they trust, and how they run the trial period and decide to keep or release the person. The gap is wider for service ventures, whose founders' resources may work differently from those of innovation-driven start-ups, and for Russia after 2022, where a surplus of applicants, the self-employment regime and founder self-funding change the conditions of the decision.

**Research problem.** Founders of early-stage marketing ventures in Russia make first-hire decisions that strongly affect the quality of service, the founder's own time and the culture of the future team, yet they do so without an evidence-based method. They rely on rules learned from their own mistakes, which is costly and slow.

The research questions stated in the Introduction follow from this. RQ1 addresses the criteria that remain unclear because of the pay and labour-market tensions. RQ2 addresses channels, where the network findings conflict. RQ3 addresses the role of the founder's experience and resources, where imprinting and learning accounts disagree, and extends the decision to onboarding and early exit. The main question asks for the model that connects them.

**Theoretical lens.** The study will use effectuation (Sarasvathy, 2001) and the simple-rules view of heuristics (Bingham and Eisenhardt, 2011) as its main lens, with imprinting (Baron and Hannan, 2002; Ahn and Greve, 2025) as a second layer. Effectuation explains why founders start from the people within reach. The simple-rules view explains how they turn failed and successful hires into rules. Imprinting explains why founders with corporate experience and first-time founders start from different rules. The combination also matches the intended output: a decision model whose stages are governed by a small set of rules that a founder can apply.

## 3. Preliminary plan of empirical study

### 3.1 Methodology

The study will use a qualitative, inductive design based on semi-structured interviews with founders. Three reasons support this choice. First, the research questions ask *how* founders decide, and the literature offers no tested model of that process. Inductive qualitative research is the usual route for building theory where constructs are not yet defined (Eisenhardt, 1989; Gioia, Corley and Hamilton, 2013). Second, the object is a sequence of choices over time, from noticing the need for help to keeping or releasing a person. Process questions call for data that preserve order and context (Langley, 1999), which a survey of practices would flatten. Third, Harney and Alkhalaf (2021) criticise small-firm HRM research for reporting managers' general views; interviews built around specific hiring episodes reduce that risk.

The data will be analysed with the Gioia methodology (Gioia et al., 2013): first-order codes close to founders' own words, second-order themes, and aggregate dimensions that form the stages of the decision model. Process mapping (Langley, 1999) will be used to order the stages and to show where founders loop back, for example from the trial period to a new search. Comparison between experienced and first-time founders will test the imprinting and learning accounts from Section 2.4.

The author's own experience as a founder is both a resource and a risk. It helps in building the interview guide and in gaining access, but it may lead to reading founders' accounts through the author's own rules. The author's experience will therefore be used only to design the guide and as an explicit reference point in analytic memos, his own agency will be excluded from the sample, and the coding will be checked by a second reader where possible.

### 3.2 Data collection

**Sample.** Purposive sampling of 10 to 14 founders of marketing-service ventures in Russia: agencies, SMM and content studios, and production projects. Inclusion criteria: the venture was at most two years old at the time of the hires discussed, and the founder made at least two hiring decisions, counting staff, freelancers, self-employed contractors and partners. The sample will be split roughly evenly between founders with prior corporate or entrepreneurial experience and first-time founders, including students, so that the two groups can be compared. Guest, Bunce and Johnson (2006) found that most themes in fairly homogeneous samples emerge within the first twelve interviews; recruitment will continue until new interviews add no new codes. Access is available through the author's professional network, and snowball referrals will be used to reach founders outside it.

**Interviews.** Interviews of 60 to 75 minutes will be held online and recorded with consent. The guide has four blocks: the founder's background and the venture; the first roles (why these roles, why an assistant, a specialist or a partner); two or three concrete hiring episodes, including at least one that failed, reconstructed with the critical incident technique (Flanagan, 1954); and the founder's current rules for hiring, onboarding and parting ways. Within each episode the founder will be asked about the trigger, the channel, the assessment tools (test task, text and video interviews, paid trial period, training before start, AI tools if any), the first weeks, and the outcome.

**Supporting data.** Where founders agree, job posts, test tasks and onboarding materials will be collected to compare stated and actual practice. Secondary data on the labour market and the industry (HeadHunter, AKAR, the Federal Tax Service) will be used to describe the context.

**Ethics.** Founders and ventures will be anonymised, and details that could identify former employees will be removed from transcripts.

### 3.3 Expected findings

**Result.** The study is expected to produce a process model of the first-hire decision in early-stage marketing ventures. A tentative sequence, to be revised by the data, is: trigger (workload or a quality risk the founder can no longer cover), choice of role type (assistant, specialist or partner), choice of channel, assessment, paid trial and onboarding, decision to keep or release, and the update of the founder's rules. For each stage the study will identify the rules founders use and how they differ between experienced and first-time founders.

**Knowledge contribution.** The study will (1) open the decision process that existing work on early hiring treats as a black box; (2) test whether the role of founder resources found in innovation-driven start-ups (Rocha and Grilli, 2024) holds in service ventures; (3) connect the imprinting and learning accounts of founders' people practices; (4) treat selection, onboarding and early exit as one decision; and (5) provide evidence from Russia after 2022, a setting missing from the international literature.

**Organisational outcomes.** Following the supervisor's research programme, which links talent management to organisational results, the study will also explore how founders judge whether a first hire succeeded. Candidate indicators are the share of founder time released, the time until the new person works independently, the share of hires released during the trial period, and client retention or service quality in the area the person took over. The final set of indicators will be agreed with the supervisor before fieldwork.

**Managerial implications.** The model will be turned into a practical framework for founders: a short set of rules and questions for each stage, from defining the first role to the end of the trial period. A shorter version can help investors and accelerators judge how a founding team builds its team.
